Trump Administration Weighs Federal Child-Care Payments for Some Stay-at-Home Parents

National

Published: Monday, September 7, 2026

WASHINGTON — The Trump administration is considering a new approach to federal child-care assistance that could allow certain married families to receive government funding even when one parent stays home to care for the children.

The proposal, which is still being developed, would redirect money from the existing Child Care and Development Fund (CCDF) to provide financial assistance to qualifying families with a stay-at-home parent.

Reports indicate that the potential benefit could amount to approximately $9,000 per child each year, although the amount is based on current child-care subsidy levels and is not a guaranteed payment under an approved program.

A Different Approach to Child-Care Assistance

The federal child-care fund was established to help low-income and working families cover the cost of child care. In most cases, the government assistance is used to pay child-care providers.

The proposed change would broaden how those dollars could be used by allowing some parents who provide child care themselves to potentially receive the benefit directly.

Under the plan being considered, a family would generally need to have two married parents, with one spouse working at least 35 hours a week while the other parent remains home to care for the children.

Income restrictions would also apply, with eligibility potentially varying depending on the state.

Single parents who are not working and unmarried couples with a stay-at-home parent would not qualify under the proposal as currently described.

Funding Could Become a Major Issue

The potential expansion has raised questions about whether the existing child-care budget could support additional recipients.

The CCDF has a finite amount of funding, and the vast majority of families currently receiving assistance are headed by single working parents. Federal data cited in reports indicate that roughly 80% of participating families are headed by a single working parent, most commonly a mother.

Child-care advocates have warned that adding another group of eligible families without increasing federal funding could put additional pressure on an already limited system.

Critics argue that families already waiting for assistance could face longer delays if stay-at-home parents are allowed to compete for the same resources.

Supporters Say Parents Shouldn’t Be Penalized for Staying Home

Backers of the proposal view the issue differently.

They argue that parents who leave the workforce to raise their children provide a form of child care themselves and currently receive little or no comparable federal assistance for doing so.

Supporters say the existing system effectively subsidizes families who pay outside child-care providers while offering nothing similar to parents who choose to provide care at home.

The proposal has also become part of a broader administration effort focused on family policy, marriage and encouraging Americans to have more children.

Vice President JD Vance has previously advocated policies that provide greater support for parents who choose to raise young children at home.

Marriage Requirement Draws Attention

One of the most notable elements of the proposal is its reported marriage requirement.

Administration officials have reportedly discussed whether limiting the benefit to married couples could withstand legal scrutiny.

That could become an important issue if the administration moves forward with the plan, particularly because federal benefit programs must comply with constitutional and statutory requirements governing eligibility.

There are also questions surrounding how the government would verify that parents are actually providing child care at home and how direct payments could affect the existing subsidy system.

No Payments Have Been Approved Yet

Despite reports describing a potential $9,000-per-child benefit, families should not expect to receive checks immediately.

The administration has not finalized the proposal, and the rules could still change.

If the White House approves the plan and moves forward through the federal regulatory process, the proposal would be published for public review and comment before potentially becoming official.

The earliest implementation could reportedly occur in 2027.

For now, the proposal remains under consideration — meaning the amount of assistance, eligibility requirements and even the final structure of the program could all change before it reaches American families.
