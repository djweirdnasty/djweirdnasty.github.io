Uber Leaves Nigeria After 12 Years as Costs, Competition and Corporate Changes Reshape Ride-Hailing

National | World

Published: Thursday, September 3, 2026

Uber is no longer operating its ride-hailing service in Nigeria, ending a 12-year run in one of Africa's largest transportation markets.

The company stopped accepting ride requests in Nigeria and Uganda on September 2, 2026, as part of a wider reassessment of its international business. Uber said the decision followed a review of its operations but emphasized that it is not leaving the African market altogether.

The company's departure marks the end of an era for Nigerian passengers and drivers who have used the platform since Uber launched in Lagos in 2014.

A Difficult Market for Ride-Hailing Companies

Uber's exit comes at a time when operating a vehicle in Nigeria has become significantly more expensive.

Fuel prices have climbed sharply since the government ended its long-running fuel subsidy in 2023. Inflation and changes in the value of the Nigerian naira have added another layer of financial pressure for both drivers and passengers.

For drivers, the higher cost of fuel, maintenance and other vehicle expenses has made earning money through ride-hailing platforms more difficult. Drivers have previously organized protests and work stoppages over fares and platform commissions.

Those challenges have affected the wider industry rather than Uber alone.

Rivals Ready to Compete for Uber's Customers

Uber's departure leaves competitors with an opportunity to capture passengers and drivers who previously depended on the platform.

Bolt and inDrive are among the major international competitors operating in Nigeria, while local ride-hailing companies also compete in the market.

Unlike Uber's traditional pricing structure, some competing services have attempted to appeal to Nigerian customers through different pricing and fare-negotiation approaches.

The result could be an increasingly competitive battle for Uber's former customer base.

The Timing Is Significant for Uber

Nigeria's exit comes as Uber makes major changes to its business worldwide.

The company has announced plans to eliminate approximately 3,300 positions, or about 10% of its global workforce. Uber says the restructuring is designed to simplify its organization and allow it to put more resources into areas with greater long-term potential.

Autonomous transportation is one of those areas.

Uber has been increasing its involvement in the development and deployment of self-driving transportation, including robotaxi technology. Rather than building its future entirely around traditional drivers, the company is positioning its platform to potentially connect passengers with autonomous vehicle operators.

That strategy could become increasingly important as self-driving transportation expands.

Nigeria Is Not Uber's Only African Departure

The Nigerian withdrawal follows other recent changes to Uber's African footprint.

The company has also stopped operating in Ivory Coast and Tanzania, while Uganda is now leaving the platform alongside Nigeria.

Uber says those decisions should not be interpreted as a withdrawal from the entire continent.

The company continues to operate in Egypt, Ghana, Kenya and South Africa, according to the BBC.

Airport Dispute Adds to the Recent Pressure

Uber's Nigerian exit also comes shortly after tensions surrounding e-hailing services at Nigerian airports.

The Federal Airports Authority of Nigeria has been involved in discussions with ride-hailing companies regarding issues such as airport pickups, security and accountability.

However, Uber has not said that the airport dispute caused its decision to leave Nigeria. The disagreement is therefore better understood as part of the challenging business environment surrounding e-hailing companies rather than a confirmed reason for Uber's withdrawal.

What Happens Next?

Uber says it will continue assisting affected drivers and customers after the shutdown. Its support center is expected to remain available in Nigeria and Uganda through September 23 for unresolved account and other issues.

For Nigerian riders, the immediate impact will likely be a greater reliance on competing platforms. For drivers, the departure could intensify competition among ride-hailing companies seeking to recruit experienced drivers and attract Uber's former customers.

Uber's exit ultimately reflects a broader shift in the transportation industry: rising operating costs are putting pressure on traditional ride-hailing models at the same time technology companies are investing heavily in autonomous vehicles.

After more than a decade in Nigeria, Uber is leaving the market—but the competition for the country's riders is far from over.
