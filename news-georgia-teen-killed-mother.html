Georgia Teen Sentenced to Life After Killing Mother Instead of Carrying Out Planned School Attack

National | Crime

Published: Friday, August 28, 2026

A Georgia teenager who prosecutors say had been considering a mass shooting at her former middle school instead killed her mother in 2024. Nearly two years later, the girl, now 16, has been sentenced to life in prison.

Lilyana Rose Wagner was 14 when Rachel Suzanne Wagner, 51, was found fatally shot inside the family’s Tifton home on Nov. 19, 2024. Wagner recently pleaded guilty to felony murder and aggravated assault in connection with her mother’s death.

Under the sentence, Wagner must serve at least 30 years before she can become eligible for parole.

The case initially came to authorities after a seemingly unrelated crash

Wagner had been involved in a single-vehicle wreck in Crawford County and told responding officers that she lived with her mother. When officers learned that Rachel Wagner had failed to report to work and could not be reached, they went to the residence to check on her.

Inside the home, investigators discovered Rachel Wagner dead in her bed. She had suffered a gunshot wound to the back of her head.

The subsequent investigation uncovered information that prosecutors described as deeply concerning.

Investigation uncovered online activity

Investigators with the Tifton Police Department and Georgia Bureau of Investigation examined data from Wagner’s phone and uncovered online conversations involving anonymous individuals who prosecutors said encouraged her to plan and commit acts of violence.

Authorities said Wagner had developed an extensive online presence centered around school shootings and mass-casualty attacks. Prosecutors characterized her interest as more than passive curiosity, saying she had cultivated a reputation online as someone interested in planning such an attack.

According to the district attorney’s office, Wagner continued communicating with some of the people she had been speaking with even after her mother’s death.

Investigators also learned that at least one of the applications she used lacked systems designed to identify and flag certain threatening language or concerning content. Prosecutors said that meant conversations potentially relevant to an emerging threat were not automatically detected and referred for investigation.

The circumstances surrounding the online contacts remain part of the broader questions raised by the case.

A handwritten confession

Wagner eventually provided investigators with a handwritten account of what happened.

In that confession, she said she had originally considered carrying out a shooting at her former middle school. She described abandoning that plan and killing her mother instead.

She also referenced six school shooters whom she said had influenced her thinking.

Prosecutors have not publicly identified all of the individuals she referenced in the confession.

The district attorney’s office said the evidence demonstrated how online influences, access to firearms and warning signs can intersect before a violent crime occurs.

Questions about access to the gun

Investigators also traced the firearm used in Rachel Wagner’s killing to a Valdosta pawn shop.

According to prosecutors, Rachel Wagner purchased the firearm approximately one month before her death. Authorities said she subsequently allowed her daughter to have access to the pistol and ammunition in the teenager’s bedroom.

That detail became one of several issues highlighted by District Attorney Patrick Warren when discussing the case and the decisions that preceded the killing.

Warren said the tragedy raises difficult questions about what might have happened if different choices had been made — including whether the teenager would have been influenced differently without the online contacts and whether the shooting could have been prevented if she had not been able to access a firearm.

Prosecutor warns about online influence

Warren used the case to issue a broader warning to parents about the influence children can encounter through online communities.

He urged parents to pay attention to who their children communicate with, what messages they are receiving and whether online behavior suggests that a young person may be moving toward dangerous beliefs or actions.

The prosecutor also stressed the importance of addressing warning signs before they become criminal conduct.

The case is particularly striking because the attack Wagner described planning at her former school never occurred. Instead, the violence was redirected toward her own family.

Rachel Wagner’s death was ultimately discovered because her daughter crashed her vehicle and officers began asking questions about why her mother was unreachable.

Sentence closes one chapter, but leaves larger questions

Wagner’s guilty plea brings the criminal case to a significant conclusion, but prosecutors say the circumstances surrounding the killing illustrate a series of missed opportunities for intervention.

Warren described the outcome as one in which nobody truly benefited: Rachel Wagner lost her life, while her daughter faces decades behind bars.

The teenager will be eligible for parole only after completing 30 years of her sentence.

For investigators and prosecutors, the case has become a warning about the combination of online radicalization toward violence, access to firearms and the difficulty of identifying threats before they turn into real-world harm.

The tragedy also underscores how a potential mass-casualty attack can evolve in unpredictable ways. In this case, authorities say a planned school attack was abandoned — but the violent intent did not disappear. Instead, it culminated in the killing of a mother inside her own home.
