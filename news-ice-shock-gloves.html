ICE Awards $16.7 Million Contract for Electric Shock Gloves Amid Growing Concerns Over Use of Force

National | Politics

Published: Thursday, August 27, 2026

U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement has awarded a multimillion-dollar contract for thousands of wearable devices capable of delivering an electric shock, drawing sharp criticism from lawmakers and civil rights advocates who are questioning how the technology will be used during immigration enforcement operations.

Federal procurement records show ICE entered into a no-bid agreement with Compliant Technologies LLC, a Kentucky-based company that manufactures the devices. The contract is valued at approximately $16.7 million and covers 6,000 gloves along with related equipment and services over a six-month period.

The purchase represents a significant expansion of a technology that has previously been used by some local police agencies and detention facilities.

ICE describes the devices as “conductive distraction and de-escalation devices” and argues that they could provide officers with another option for controlling people who resist arrest or detention without immediately resorting to more severe forms of force.

The agency’s decision has nevertheless prompted questions about safeguards, training and accountability, particularly as ICE continues to face scrutiny over its enforcement tactics.

How the Gloves Work

The device, known as the G.L.O.V.E., or Generated Low Output Voltage Emitter, resembles a conventional law enforcement glove.

According to information released about the technology, an officer can activate an electrical mode and deliver a shock through direct physical contact with another person’s skin.

The stated purpose is to create a painful distraction intended to gain compliance from someone who is resisting.

ICE says the gloves could be used during arrests, detainee transportation and disturbances near detention facilities. Procurement documents describe potential situations involving people who are actively or passively resisting and where officers believe they need to gain control quickly.

The agency has argued that providing officers with an intermediate tool could reduce the need for physical struggles and, in some circumstances, prevent situations from escalating toward more serious weapons.

ICE said the devices would be deployed according to agency policies, training requirements and accountability procedures.

However, lawmakers challenging the purchase say the agency has not publicly provided enough information about those safeguards.

Lawmakers Demand Answers

The planned purchase has become a point of contention in Congress.

Sen. Catherine Cortez Masto of Nevada and 15 other Democratic senators called on ICE to abandon the acquisition, arguing that electric-shock technology creates significant risks when used during civil immigration enforcement.

The senators questioned whether officers would have clear limits governing when the gloves can be activated, how their use would be documented and what consequences would follow if an officer used the device improperly.

Their concerns come amid broader criticism of ICE’s use-of-force practices during the Trump administration’s immigration crackdown.

The lawmakers also questioned whether officers would receive sufficient training to distinguish genuine resistance from involuntary physical reactions caused by an electric shock.

Rep. Lauren Underwood, a Democrat who serves on the House Homeland Security Appropriations Subcommittee, separately condemned the planned expenditure.

Underwood said spending millions of taxpayer dollars on the technology was unacceptable and called on the Department of Homeland Security to halt the procurement.

Her office also raised concerns about protections for vulnerable people, noting that the manufacturer warns against using the device on elderly individuals, small children, pregnant women and people with severe disabilities.

Questions About Accountability

One of the most significant concerns raised by critics involves the visibility of the device’s use.

Underwood’s office pointed to the possibility that an electric shock could be delivered without leaving obvious burns, marks or scars.

That has prompted questions about how investigators, courts and the public would later determine whether a shock was used and whether its use complied with agency policy.

Underwood also connected the controversy to ongoing questions about body-worn camera coverage among DHS personnel.

Her office said the congresswoman had recently met with DHS officials about gaps in body-camera deployment. During those discussions, officials cited the expense associated with fully equipping ICE and Customs and Border Protection personnel with cameras.

Underwood questioned why DHS could commit up to $20 million toward electric-shock gloves while body-camera coverage remains incomplete.

The issue could become especially significant in cases where an officer’s account of an encounter differs from video footage or other evidence.

Manufacturer’s Safety Restrictions

The manufacturer has established restrictions for the device’s use.

According to its instructions, officers should provide a person with a reasonable opportunity to comply before applying force. The company recommends limiting the electrical application to a short period under normal circumstances, although the instructions allow different circumstances when a person becomes more violent or poses a danger.

The manufacturer also states that the gloves are not intended to be used as punishment.

Its safety guidance warns against using the technology on certain vulnerable populations, including young children, pregnant women, elderly people and individuals with severe disabilities.

Those restrictions have become central to the debate over ICE’s planned deployment.

Critics argue that the federal agency needs to explain exactly how those restrictions will be incorporated into field policies and how officers will be held accountable for violations.

DHS Defends the Purchase

The Department of Homeland Security has pushed back strongly against criticism.

A DHS spokesperson characterized efforts to stop the purchase as an attempt to prevent federal law enforcement personnel from obtaining equipment intended to protect officers.

The department said ICE regularly evaluates the equipment available to its personnel and maintains that its decisions are reviewed for compliance with applicable law enforcement standards.

DHS also said ICE personnel receive training in de-escalation techniques.

The department maintains that the gloves are intended to provide officers with an additional option in situations that could otherwise escalate.

ICE’s procurement documents similarly describe the devices as a way to control dangerous or resistant situations without immediately turning to firearms or other forms of more serious force.

A New Tool for an Increasingly Scrutinized Agency

The contract comes at a particularly contentious time for ICE.

The agency has dramatically expanded immigration enforcement under President Donald Trump’s administration, while its tactics have generated protests, legal challenges and congressional scrutiny.

Critics argue that adding an electrically powered pain-compliance device to the agency’s equipment could increase concerns about excessive force.

Supporters of the purchase counter that officers need alternatives between hands-on physical restraint and more dangerous weapons, particularly when confronting people who resist arrest.

The central question is whether the gloves will actually function as a de-escalation tool or whether their availability could make the use of force easier in situations where other methods might have been appropriate.

The Financial Commitment

Federal records indicate that the government agreed to purchase 6,000 devices at a price of approximately $2,495 per glove, with the overall agreement reaching about $16.7 million when equipment, support and services are included.

The arrangement is structured for six months.

Earlier procurement documents had indicated that the potential value of the acquisition could reach roughly $20 million, depending on the terms and additional requirements.

That price tag has intensified criticism from lawmakers who believe the agency should prioritize other needs.

Underwood specifically cited body-camera deployment as an area where federal resources could be directed instead.

ICE’s Next Challenge: Establishing Trust

The immediate dispute is no longer about whether ICE intends to acquire the technology. The agency has now awarded the contract.

The debate is shifting toward how the devices will be deployed and whether the public will have sufficient visibility into their use.

Questions remain over the precise circumstances that will justify activating a glove, how officers will document each use, whether supervisors will review incidents and what medical or investigative procedures will follow complaints of misuse.

Those questions could become particularly important because the technology is designed to cause pain without necessarily producing visible physical injuries.

For ICE, the gloves are being presented as another tool intended to help officers control difficult encounters while reducing the need for more serious force.

For critics, however, the purchase represents another expansion of an agency already facing substantial questions about its use of force.

The controversy is likely to continue as ICE moves from procurement to implementation and lawmakers seek greater transparency over the policies governing when and how federal immigration officers can use the devices.
