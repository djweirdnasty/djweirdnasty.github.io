FDA Approves Rasonque, Opening a New Treatment Era for Advanced Pancreatic Cancer

National | Health & Medicine

Published: Wednesday, August 26, 2026

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has approved Rasonque, a new oral cancer treatment that could significantly change the way advanced pancreatic cancer is treated after previous therapies stop working.

The drug, known generically as daraxonrasib, was developed by Revolution Medicines and is approved for adults with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma who have received at least one previous systemic treatment or who are not candidates for multi-agent systemic therapy.

The approval comes after results from a large Phase 3 clinical trial showed a substantial survival advantage over standard chemotherapy. In the RASolute 302 trial, patients receiving Rasonque had a median overall survival of 13.2 months, compared with 6.7 months among patients receiving chemotherapy.

The results have been described by cancer specialists and pancreatic cancer advocates as a major development for a disease that has historically been among the most difficult cancers to treat.

A Different Approach to Pancreatic Cancer

Pancreatic cancer is often diagnosed after it has already spread, leaving patients with fewer treatment options. Traditional chemotherapy can slow the disease, but advanced pancreatic cancer frequently becomes resistant to treatment.

Rasonque takes a different approach.

The once-daily pill is designed to interfere with RAS signaling, a biological pathway that controls cell growth. Mutations involving RAS can cause the pathway to remain continuously active, sending signals that encourage cancer cells to multiply and survive.

The RAS pathway has been a major focus of cancer research for decades. Scientists have long considered several RAS proteins difficult to target effectively with drugs.

Daraxonrasib represents a new effort to overcome that challenge.

The treatment is designed to inhibit RAS signaling and was developed with the goal of targeting a biological driver behind many pancreatic tumors.

Clinical Trial Shows Nearly Double the Survival

The FDA’s decision was supported by results from the RASolute 302 Phase 3 trial, which enrolled approximately 500 patients with previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer.

Patients were assigned to receive either Rasonque or a chemotherapy regimen selected by their doctors.

The difference in overall survival was striking.

Patients treated with Rasonque survived a median of 13.2 months, while those receiving standard chemotherapy had a median overall survival of 6.7 months.

The study also showed improvements in other measures of disease control. Patients receiving Rasonque generally experienced longer periods before their cancer progressed.

Researchers also reported improvements in quality-of-life measures and pain control among patients receiving the targeted treatment.

Those findings helped fuel enthusiasm among oncologists who specialize in gastrointestinal cancers.

The results were presented at major medical meetings and published in medical literature, providing additional evidence as regulators evaluated the treatment.

Side Effects Can Be Significant

The drug’s benefits come with potential side effects that patients and physicians will need to carefully manage.

Among the most frequently reported complications are skin rash, mouth sores and diarrhea.

Data from the clinical trial showed that roughly 86% of patients developed a rash, while approximately 14% experienced severe cases. Mouth sores, medically called stomatitis, were reported in about 54% of participants, with roughly 12% experiencing severe symptoms.

Doctors can use dose interruptions and reductions to help manage some adverse reactions.

Despite the frequency of side effects, permanent discontinuation because of treatment-related complications was relatively uncommon in the clinical trial, occurring in approximately 1.2% of patients.

Supportive care will therefore be an important part of treatment. Patients may need medications and other measures to control rashes, mouth irritation and gastrointestinal symptoms while remaining on therapy.

Patients Had Access Before Full Approval

Wednesday’s approval follows an expanded-access program that allowed some patients with advanced pancreatic cancer to receive daraxonrasib while the treatment was still under regulatory review.

Revolution Medicines began the program earlier this year, eventually providing the drug to thousands of patients who otherwise might not have had access to it outside a clinical trial.

Now that the FDA has granted approval, eligible patients can receive Rasonque through standard commercial channels.

The company has also announced a patient-support program intended to assist with treatment education, insurance questions and financial resources.

The treatment will be taken as a daily oral medication rather than administered through an intravenous infusion, giving patients an alternative to some traditional chemotherapy delivery methods.

The Drug Is Not a Cure

Despite the excitement surrounding the FDA decision, Rasonque does not represent a cure for pancreatic cancer.

The approval applies to a specific group of patients with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma. Some patients will respond better than others, and cancers can eventually develop resistance to targeted treatments.

Researchers are already studying whether Rasonque can provide benefits in other circumstances.

Clinical trials are examining combinations involving chemotherapy and other targeted medicines. Scientists are also investigating whether the drug could eventually have a role earlier in the disease, including treatment strategies designed to reduce the chances of pancreatic cancer returning after surgery.

Those studies could ultimately broaden the role of Rasonque if they demonstrate additional benefits.

Why RAS Matters

The importance of the approval extends beyond pancreatic cancer.

RAS proteins are involved in controlling cell growth and division, and abnormal RAS signaling plays a role in numerous cancers. Mutations involving KRAS, a member of the RAS family, are particularly common in pancreatic tumors.

For decades, researchers struggled to turn knowledge about these mutations into effective treatments.

That began changing as scientists developed new approaches for targeting RAS-driven cancers.

Rasonque’s approval represents another major step in that effort and could encourage the development of additional medicines aimed at the same biological pathway.

A Potential Turning Point

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the deadliest major cancers, in part because it frequently produces few obvious symptoms during its earliest stages.

There is also no routine screening test for the general population comparable to screening programs used for several other cancers. As a result, many patients are diagnosed only after the disease has advanced.

That makes the availability of an effective second-line treatment particularly significant.

The Rasonque trial did not eliminate the challenges associated with pancreatic cancer, but the difference between 13.2 months and 6.7 months of median overall survival represents a meaningful advance for patients whose disease has already progressed.

For oncologists, the FDA decision also signals that targeting the biological mechanisms driving pancreatic cancer may finally be producing substantial clinical benefits.

For patients and families, the approval offers another treatment option at a stage of the disease where choices have historically been limited.

The next phase of research will focus on determining which patients benefit most, how long those benefits can be sustained and whether Rasonque can be combined with other treatments to produce even stronger results.

For now, the FDA approval marks a significant milestone in pancreatic cancer treatment and a major step forward in the decades-long effort to successfully target the RAS pathway.
