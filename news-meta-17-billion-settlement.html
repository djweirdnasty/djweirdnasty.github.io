Meta Agrees to $17 Billion Settlement With States Over Alleged Harm to Children

National | Technology

Published: Wednesday, August 26, 2026

OAKLAND, Calif. — Meta has agreed to a proposed settlement worth up to $17 billion with dozens of U.S. states and jurisdictions over allegations that Facebook and Instagram were deliberately designed in ways that encouraged excessive use among children and teenagers.

The agreement, announced Wednesday, brings an early end to a closely watched federal trial in California that could have continued for weeks. The case centered on accusations that Meta violated consumer-protection laws and federal children’s privacy requirements while failing to adequately address risks associated with its platforms.

Under the proposed agreement, Meta would pay approximately $17 billion over 10 years and implement a broad collection of new safeguards aimed at limiting how young people use Facebook and Instagram.

Settlement Comes During Second Week of Trial

The settlement follows years of litigation and comes just days after the trial began in federal court in Oakland.

California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey were among the states leading the case, while the broader settlement involves 47 states, the District of Columbia and U.S. territories. Separate litigation had also been filed by other states, school districts and individuals.

The states accused Meta of developing features intended to keep young users engaged for extended periods while allegedly understating or concealing information about potential risks to children’s well-being.

Meta has denied the allegations.

The case had attracted considerable attention because executives and former employees were expected to provide testimony about the company’s approach to youth safety. Instagram chief Adam Mosseri testified shortly before the settlement was announced, while CEO Mark Zuckerberg had been expected to testify later in the proceedings.

New Restrictions Would Change How Teens Use Instagram and Facebook

The proposed settlement would require Meta to introduce or strengthen several protections for users under 18.

Among the changes described by state officials are:

  • A two-hour default daily usage limit for teens on Instagram and Facebook.
  • A midnight-to-6 a.m. nighttime restriction that parents can override.
  • Automatic restrictions on notifications during overnight hours.
  • Reduced or eliminated notifications during typical school hours.
  • Stronger age-verification and age-assurance systems.
  • Expanded parental controls.
  • Restrictions on displaying like and reaction counts to minors.
  • Limits on certain cosmetic or appearance-related filters.
  • Greater controls over potentially harmful content.
  • An option allowing young users to receive a non-personalized feed rather than one driven by individualized recommendations.
  • Measures designed to interrupt continuous scrolling and encourage users to take breaks.

The agreement would also place Meta under additional scrutiny. An independent auditor would monitor the company’s implementation of the safeguards and evaluate their effectiveness.

Meta would additionally face restrictions against making false, misleading or deceptive statements concerning the effectiveness of its youth-safety features.

Billions Could Depend on Other Social Media Companies

The settlement’s ultimate value could depend on whether competing social media companies agree to similar terms.

Meta initially expects to pay roughly $12 billion, with additional payments potentially bringing the total to approximately $17 billion.

Meta has urged companies including TikTok, YouTube and Snap to adopt comparable protections.

The company has argued that teenagers routinely move between multiple platforms, meaning that restrictions imposed on only one service could have limited effectiveness.

“Teens move fluidly across dozens of apps,” Meta Chief Legal Officer C.J. Mahoney said in announcing the company’s position on the framework.

The other companies have not agreed to the same settlement.

States Had Sought Much Larger Penalties

The financial stakes in the litigation were potentially enormous.

The states involved in the Oakland case had been seeking roughly $200 billion, according to reporting surrounding the trial. Meta had previously warned in a court filing that potential penalties could theoretically reach an even higher figure, although such an outcome was considered unlikely.

The settlement therefore represents a negotiated resolution rather than a jury verdict determining the maximum damages sought by the states.

California Attorney General Rob Bonta said California would receive at least $1.5 billion under the agreement, with payments distributed over a decade.

Other states are also expected to receive substantial amounts.

The money is expected to support initiatives including youth mental-health programs, school programs, crisis-intervention efforts and other measures related to online safety.

Lawsuits Focused on Children’s Mental Health

The litigation is part of a much larger legal fight over the impact of social media on young people.

State attorneys general argued that Meta knew its products could expose children to harmful experiences while continuing to prioritize engagement and the amount of time users spent on its services.

Former Meta employee Arturo Béjar was among the witnesses who testified during the trial. He accused the company of placing business priorities ahead of concerns about the experiences young users were having on its platforms.

The states also pointed to internal research and testimony concerning issues including body image, harmful content and compulsive use.

The legal campaign has drawn comparisons to earlier litigation against tobacco companies, although the technology companies have strongly disputed the comparison.

Privacy Allegations Also Played a Role

The case wasn’t limited to allegations involving addictive design.

The states also accused Meta of violating the Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act, commonly known as COPPA, by collecting information from children under 13 without appropriate parental consent.

Meta prohibits children under 13 from having accounts, but regulators and lawmakers have long questioned whether age restrictions are effective when children can circumvent them by providing false birth dates.

The settlement would require Meta to increase its efforts to determine users’ ages and strengthen protections for younger users.

Meta Still Faces Other Legal Battles

Wednesday’s agreement does not end all of Meta’s legal exposure.

The company continues to face lawsuits brought by school districts, families and individual young people alleging that its products contributed to mental-health problems and other harms.

Meta has indicated that it intends to continue fighting many of those cases.

The company also recently faced significant legal setbacks in other litigation involving youth safety. Those cases could provide additional tests of how courts treat claims that social media companies should be held responsible for the design and consequences of their products.

Texas Reaches Separate Agreement

Separately, Meta agreed to a settlement with Texas worth approximately $1 billion over similar allegations.

That agreement includes additional product changes and funding for youth-related programs and school initiatives in the state.

Combined with the federal settlement involving the other states, Meta’s agreements with state governments could therefore exceed $18 billion.

A Potential Turning Point for Big Tech

The settlement could become an important moment in the growing legal campaign against social media companies over youth safety.

For years, technology companies have faced criticism from parents, lawmakers and child-safety advocates over features such as infinite scrolling, personalized recommendations, autoplay, notifications and public engagement metrics.

Some of those features have already been modified in response to regulatory pressure, particularly in Europe.

The new agreement would accelerate similar changes in the United States and establish enforceable requirements for Meta’s platforms.

The settlement still requires approval from U.S. District Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers before it becomes final.

If approved, the agreement will bring the landmark Oakland case to a close while leaving unresolved questions about how courts and lawmakers will regulate the broader social-media industry and whether competing platforms will adopt comparable restrictions.

The proposed settlement represents one of the largest financial agreements ever reached between a major technology company and U.S. states over alleged consumer and child-safety violations.
