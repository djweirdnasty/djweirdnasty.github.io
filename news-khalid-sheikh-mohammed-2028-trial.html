Khalid Sheikh Mohammed Set for 2028 Trial in Long-Running 9/11 Case

National | Terrorism

Published: Friday, August 28, 2026

More than two decades after the September 11 terrorist attacks, the U.S. military has set a new trial date for Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, the alleged principal planner of the attacks.

A military judge has ordered that proceedings against Mohammed and three other defendants begin June 5, 2028, pushing the long-delayed case further into the future as attorneys continue to resolve disputes over evidence and other pretrial matters.

The decision was issued Wednesday by Air Force Lt. Col. Michael A. Schrama. Prosecutors had requested that the trial begin in 2027, but the judge determined that additional time was necessary.

Mohammed has been held at the U.S. detention facility at Guantanamo Bay, Cuba, since 2006. He was captured in Pakistan in 2003.

A case that has been delayed for years

The prosecution has been slowed by extensive legal disputes, particularly over evidence obtained while Mohammed and other defendants were held at secret CIA detention facilities.

Defense attorneys have argued that statements made by the accused during that period were tainted by coercive interrogation and torture. Questions surrounding whether such evidence can be used at trial have remained a central issue in the case.

Mohammed has previously claimed responsibility for planning the September 11 operation, saying he was involved in developing the plot and presenting the concept to al-Qaeda leader Osama bin Laden.

Prosecutors allege that Mohammed helped develop the plan to use hijacked commercial aircraft as weapons against targets in the United States.

The other defendants scheduled to face trial with him are Walid bin Attash, Ali Abdul Aziz Ali and Mustafa al-Hawsawi.

Plea agreement collapsed

The case appeared to take a major turn in 2024 when Mohammed and two other defendants reached an agreement with military prosecutors.

Under the proposed deal, the defendants would plead guilty to charges stemming from the attacks in exchange for prosecutors removing the possibility of the death penalty. Mohammed would have received a life sentence at Guantanamo Bay.

The agreement generated opposition from some relatives of people killed in the attacks.

Then-Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin subsequently moved to withdraw the agreement, arguing that a decision of such significance should have required his approval as the senior authority overseeing the military commissions.

The defendants challenged that action in court.

A federal appeals court in Washington later ruled that Austin had the authority to revoke the agreement, leaving the prosecution to continue toward a potential trial.

Nearly 25 years after the attacks

The new trial date comes as the United States approaches the 25th anniversary of the September 11 attacks.

On Sept. 11, 2001, terrorists hijacked four commercial airplanes as part of a coordinated attack. Two aircraft were flown into New York City’s World Trade Center towers, while another struck the Pentagon in Virginia.

A fourth plane crashed in Pennsylvania after passengers attempted to regain control of the aircraft.

Nearly 3,000 people were killed.

The attacks fundamentally changed U.S. national security policy and led to major military and intelligence operations targeting al-Qaeda and other terrorist organizations.

A trial still years away

The June 2028 date represents another milestone in a case that has spent years moving through military and federal courts.

The judge’s decision to postpone the proceedings reflects the continuing work required before a trial can begin, including resolving arguments over evidence and other procedural questions.

If the schedule holds, the proceedings will begin nearly 27 years after the September 11 attacks and roughly 25 years after Mohammed’s capture.

For victims’ families and others who have followed the case for decades, the new date provides a formal timetable for a trial that has repeatedly been delayed by legal challenges, disputed evidence and the complicated history of the defendants’ detention and interrogation.
