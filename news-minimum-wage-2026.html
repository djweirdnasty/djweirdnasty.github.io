Minimum Wage Across All 50 States in 2026: What Workers Are Earning

National | Economy

Published: Wednesday, August 13, 2026

Minimum wage laws aren’t the same everywhere in the United States. While the federal government continues to set a baseline wage of $7.25 an hour, many states have adopted higher rates for workers.

Some states have no separate minimum wage and rely on federal law, while others have rates that are more than twice the federal minimum. Certain states also use different rates based on location, employer size, industry or type of employment.

The following is a state-by-state look at the minimum wage rates reported by the U.S. Department of Labor as of July 1, 2026.

2026 Minimum Wage Rates by State

Alabama — $7.25
Alabama does not have its own state minimum wage. Workers covered by federal wage law generally must receive at least $7.25 per hour.

Alaska — $14.00
Alaska’s minimum wage is $14 per hour. The state also has overtime provisions that can require additional pay after certain daily or weekly hours.

Arizona — $15.15
Arizona’s minimum wage is $15.15 per hour.

Arkansas — $11.00
Arkansas requires covered employers with at least four employees to pay a minimum of $11 per hour.

California — $16.90
California’s statewide minimum wage is $16.90 per hour. The state also has some of the country’s more extensive overtime rules, including circumstances where overtime can apply based on hours worked in a single day.

Colorado — $15.16
Colorado’s minimum wage is $15.16 per hour. Overtime rules can apply after 12 hours in a day or 40 hours during a workweek for covered employees.

Connecticut — $16.94
Connecticut workers are entitled to a minimum wage of $16.94 per hour under the statewide rate.

Delaware — $15.00
Delaware’s minimum wage is $15 per hour. State law also addresses situations where the federal minimum wage could become higher than the state rate.

Florida — $14.00
Florida’s minimum wage currently stands at $14 per hour.

Georgia — $7.25 for federally covered workers
Georgia has a state minimum listed at $5.15 per hour, but federal law generally supersedes that rate for employers and employees covered by the Fair Labor Standards Act. Those workers generally must receive at least $7.25 per hour.

Hawaii — $16.00
Hawaii’s minimum wage is $16 per hour. Covered workers generally qualify for overtime after 40 hours in a workweek.

Idaho — $7.25
Idaho’s minimum wage matches the federal rate at $7.25 per hour.

Illinois — $15.00
Illinois has a $15 hourly minimum wage for covered employers meeting the state’s employee threshold.

Indiana — $7.25
Indiana’s minimum wage is $7.25 per hour, with overtime generally applying after 40 hours in a workweek for covered workers.

Iowa — $7.25
Iowa’s minimum wage is $7.25 per hour and generally tracks the federal minimum when the federal rate is higher.

Kansas — $7.25
Kansas generally relies on the federal minimum wage for employment covered by federal wage law.

Kentucky — $7.25
Kentucky’s minimum wage is $7.25 per hour. The state also has separate rules concerning overtime and employees who work seven consecutive days.

Louisiana — $7.25
Louisiana does not have a separate state minimum wage. Covered workers generally fall under the federal $7.25 rate.

Maine — $15.10
Maine’s minimum wage is $15.10 per hour. The state adjusts its rate periodically using a formula tied to economic conditions.

Maryland — $15.00
Maryland’s minimum wage is $15 per hour, with overtime generally required after 40 hours in a workweek for covered employees.

Massachusetts — $15.00
Massachusetts has a statewide minimum wage of $15 per hour.

Michigan — $13.73
Michigan’s minimum wage is $13.73 per hour. The state is scheduled to move to a $15 hourly minimum on January 1, 2027, before future adjustments take effect.

Minnesota — $11.41
Minnesota’s minimum wage is $11.41 per hour. The rate is subject to periodic adjustments.

Mississippi — $7.25
Mississippi does not have a state minimum wage law. Covered employers generally must follow the federal $7.25 rate.

Missouri — $15.00
Missouri’s minimum wage is $15 per hour, although certain businesses and employment situations may qualify for exemptions.

Montana — $10.85
For businesses with annual gross sales above $110,000, Montana’s listed minimum wage is $10.85 per hour. Certain smaller businesses not covered by federal wage law can fall under a different $4 minimum under specific circumstances.

Nebraska — $15.00
Nebraska’s minimum wage is $15 per hour for covered employers with four or more employees.

Nevada — $12.00
Nevada’s minimum wage is $12 per hour. Depending on an employee’s pay rate and schedule, additional premium-pay requirements can apply.

New Hampshire — $7.25
New Hampshire’s minimum wage is $7.25 per hour and generally follows the federal rate.

New Jersey — $15.92
New Jersey’s standard minimum wage is $15.92 per hour. Certain employees working for businesses with fewer than six workers and some seasonal employees are subject to a separate $15.23 rate.

New Mexico — $12.00
New Mexico’s statewide minimum wage is $12 per hour.

New York — $17.00 or $16.00
New York uses different minimum wages depending on where an employee works. The rate is $17 per hour in New York City, Nassau County, Suffolk County and Westchester County. Workers elsewhere in the state have a minimum wage of $16 per hour.

North Carolina — $7.25
North Carolina’s minimum wage is $7.25 per hour. Certain seasonal recreational businesses have additional overtime rules.

North Dakota — $7.25
North Dakota’s minimum wage is $7.25 per hour.

Ohio — $11.00 for qualifying larger employers
Ohio requires employers with at least $405,000 in annual gross receipts to pay a minimum wage of $11 per hour. Employers below that revenue threshold have a listed minimum of $7.25.

Oklahoma — $7.25 for qualifying employers
Oklahoma’s minimum wage rules depend partly on the size and type of business. Employers covered by federal wage law generally must pay at least $7.25 per hour.

Oregon — $14.55 to $16.80
Oregon has multiple minimum wage zones. The standard rate is $15.55 per hour. Workers in the Portland metropolitan area have a minimum of $16.80. The rate in designated non-urban counties is $14.55.

Pennsylvania — $7.25
Pennsylvania’s minimum wage remains $7.25 per hour. Covered employees are generally entitled to overtime at one-and-a-half times their regular rate after working more than 40 hours during a workweek.

Rhode Island — $16.00
Rhode Island’s minimum wage is $16 per hour. Additional premium-pay rules can apply to certain Sunday and holiday work.

South Carolina — $7.25
South Carolina has no state minimum wage law. Covered employers generally fall under the federal $7.25 minimum.

South Dakota — $11.85
South Dakota’s minimum wage is $11.85 per hour and is adjusted periodically according to a state formula.

Tennessee — $7.25
Tennessee does not have a separate state minimum wage. Covered employers generally must follow the federal rate.

Texas — $7.25
Texas’ minimum wage is $7.25 per hour. State law generally follows the federal minimum for covered employment.

Utah — $7.25
Utah’s minimum wage is $7.25 per hour for covered workers.

Vermont — $14.42
Vermont’s minimum wage is $14.42 per hour for covered employers with two or more employees. The rate is adjusted annually.

Virginia — $12.77
Virginia’s minimum wage is $12.77 per hour and is subject to periodic adjustments.

Washington — $17.13
Washington has one of the nation’s highest statewide minimum wages at $17.13 per hour.

West Virginia — $8.75
West Virginia’s minimum wage is $8.75 per hour for covered employers with at least six employees at one location.

Wisconsin — $7.25
Wisconsin’s minimum wage remains $7.25 per hour.

Wyoming — $7.25 for federally covered workers
Wyoming’s state minimum is listed at $5.15 per hour, but employers covered by federal wage requirements generally must pay at least $7.25 per hour.

States With the Highest Minimum Wages

The wage gap between states is significant. Among the highest rates listed by the Department of Labor are:

  • Washington: $17.13
  • New York: $17.00 in several major counties
  • Connecticut: $16.94
  • California: $16.90
  • Oregon: Up to $16.80
  • Hawaii: $16.00
  • Rhode Island: $16.00
  • New Jersey: $15.92
  • Colorado: $15.16
  • Arizona: $15.15

Local governments may establish higher wage requirements, meaning the statewide figure isn’t always the maximum wage requirement in a particular city or county.

What $7.25 an Hour Looks Like

The federal minimum wage of $7.25 has remained unchanged for years.

Someone working 40 hours a week at $7.25 would earn $290 per week before taxes. Assuming 52 weeks of work, that comes to $15,080 per year before taxes.

By comparison, a worker earning $17 per hour and working the same 40-hour schedule would make approximately $680 per week or roughly $35,360 per year before taxes. That is a difference of more than $20,000 annually before taxes, assuming the worker maintains a 40-hour schedule for the entire year.

Minimum Wage Isn’t the Only Pay Rule

Workers should not assume that the posted minimum wage tells the entire story. Overtime, tips, local wage ordinances, industry-specific rules, employer size and exemptions can all affect what an employee is legally entitled to receive.

Federal law generally requires eligible nonexempt workers to receive overtime at one-and-a-half times their regular rate when they work more than 40 hours in a workweek. Some states provide additional protections beyond federal requirements. California, for example, has daily overtime provisions in addition to its weekly overtime rules.

Why Workers Should Check Their Local Laws

A statewide minimum wage is a useful starting point, but it may not be the final answer for every employee. A worker’s legal wage can depend on their city, county, occupation, employer, age, employment status or whether they receive tips.

For that reason, employees who believe they may be underpaid should review their pay stubs and hours worked and compare them with the wage laws that apply to their particular job.

The Bottom Line

The minimum wage landscape in the United States remains highly divided in 2026. While the federal minimum remains $7.25 an hour, workers in several states are guaranteed significantly higher pay. Washington’s statewide rate is above $17 per hour, while several other states have minimum wages of $15 or more.

For employees, understanding the minimum wage in their state is an important first step—but local and industry-specific rules can also make a difference.
