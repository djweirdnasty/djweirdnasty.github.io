Florida Officer Accused of Repeatedly Tracking Estranged Wife Through License Plate Cameras

National | Crime

Published: Tuesday, August 25, 2026

A Florida police officer is facing allegations that he misused a law-enforcement surveillance system to repeatedly check the movements of his estranged wife’s vehicle, with investigators saying the searches occurred hundreds of times over a period of nearly two years.

Christopher Goodson, 31, who served with the Haines City Police Department, is accused of using the department’s Flock Safety license plate reader system to look up his estranged wife’s vehicle 717 times between September 2024 and June 2026.

The investigation found that the searches were allegedly unrelated to any active criminal case involving the woman or her vehicle.

Goodson has been charged with official misconduct and a computer-related offense. He was placed on paid administrative leave, and authorities said he has indicated that he plans to resign from the department.

Search Activity Spanned Nearly Two Years

According to investigative documents cited in reports about the case, Goodson’s searches occurred from Sept. 1, 2024, through June 30, 2026.

The number of searches reportedly varied during that period, but investigators identified particularly heavy activity during some months. In September 2025 alone, Goodson allegedly searched the license plate approximately 280 times.

The searches allowed investigators to determine how frequently the vehicle’s information was being accessed and raised questions about why an officer was repeatedly checking the same vehicle.

Authorities determined that Goodson’s estranged wife was not the subject of a criminal investigation. Investigators also found that her vehicle was not listed as being associated with a crime that would have provided an official reason for Goodson to track its location.

Officer Said He Was Checking on His Children

The investigation reportedly came to light after Goodson’s estranged wife was contacted by a Washington Post reporter in August 2026.

According to the reported probable cause affidavit, the woman informed Goodson about the reporter’s inquiry. Goodson then reportedly went to a supervisor and acknowledged that he had used the Flock system to conduct searches involving his estranged wife’s vehicle.

Goodson allegedly explained that he was using the information to keep track of his children while they were with their mother.

That explanation became part of the investigation as officials examined whether his access to the system was authorized for that purpose. Because the database is intended for legitimate law-enforcement activity, personal use of the system can raise serious concerns about privacy and misuse of government resources.

Investigators Say Searches Included Misleading Categories

Authorities also allege that Goodson selected law-enforcement categories that did not accurately reflect the circumstances surrounding his searches.

Reported examples include classifications involving criminal motor vehicle offenses, wanted people or fugitives, and drug-related investigations.

Investigators allege these selections made the searches appear to have a legitimate police purpose even though there was no active criminal investigation involving his estranged wife or her vehicle.

The allegations have not been proven in court, and Goodson remains entitled to the presumption of innocence as the case moves forward.

Department Places Officer on Leave

Following the investigation, the Haines City Police Department placed Goodson on paid administrative leave.

Goodson joined the department in March 2022. Police officials said he has indicated that he intends to resign from his position.

Haines City Police Chief Isaac Jackson called the situation unfortunate and emphasized that department investigative systems are intended for official law-enforcement purposes.

The department said officers are prohibited from misusing investigative databases, accessing information without authorization, or using law-enforcement information for personal purposes.

Jackson also directed the department to conduct additional quarterly reviews of database usage. The goal is to make sure personnel are accessing investigative systems only when there is a legitimate law-enforcement reason.

The Technology Behind the Allegations

Flock Safety’s automated license plate reader technology is increasingly used by law-enforcement agencies to identify vehicles and assist with investigations.

The cameras can capture information about vehicles passing through their coverage areas and allow authorized users to search collected data. Investigators can use the technology to help locate vehicles associated with criminal investigations or identify possible movements relevant to a case.

However, the growing use of automated license plate readers has also sparked concerns about privacy and the potential for misuse.

The Goodson case demonstrates one of those concerns: an authorized user may have access to information that can reveal where a particular vehicle has been detected. Without appropriate restrictions and auditing, that access could potentially be used for reasons unrelated to law enforcement.

Why Database Oversight Matters

The allegations have also raised questions about how police departments monitor employee access to sensitive databases.

Modern law-enforcement systems can create detailed electronic records showing who searched for information, what information was searched, and when the search occurred. Regular audits can potentially identify unusual behavior, such as repeated searches involving the same person or vehicle.

Strong oversight can include access logging, automated alerts for unusual search patterns, supervisor reviews, and disciplinary procedures for unauthorized use.

Those safeguards are particularly important when databases contain information capable of revealing a person’s movements.

In Goodson’s case, investigators ultimately identified hundreds of searches involving his estranged wife’s vehicle. The department’s decision to add additional quarterly reviews suggests an effort to strengthen oversight of its investigative technology.

A Broader Privacy Debate

The case comes as police agencies continue expanding their use of technology capable of collecting information about vehicles and their movements.

Supporters of license plate reader systems argue that the technology can help investigators solve crimes and locate vehicles connected to criminal activity. Critics have raised concerns about how much location information can be collected and how long that information may remain available.

The allegations against Goodson put those competing concerns into a personal context. Technology designed to help law enforcement investigate crimes can become problematic when an authorized user allegedly applies it to a private dispute or personal relationship.

Goodson now faces criminal charges related to his alleged database activity. His employment with the Haines City Police Department is also coming to an end, according to authorities.

The case will ultimately be decided through the legal process, but the allegations underscore the importance of strict controls, regular audits, and accountability whenever law-enforcement agencies provide employees with access to powerful surveillance technology.
