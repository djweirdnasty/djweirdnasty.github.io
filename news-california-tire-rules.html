California Approves New Tire Rules That Will Change Replacement Options for Drivers

National News

Published: Friday, August 21, 2026

California is moving forward with new efficiency standards for replacement tires, creating the first statewide program in the United States aimed at regulating how much energy replacement tires use while vehicles are on the road.

The California Energy Commission unanimously approved the Replacement Tire Efficiency Program on August 17. The first requirements are scheduled to begin in 2029, with stricter standards arriving in 2033.

The regulations are designed to address a common difference between factory-installed tires and replacement tires. According to the California Energy Commission, tires installed on new vehicles are generally engineered with lower rolling resistance, while replacement tires can require more energy to move.

What the New Rules Mean

Rolling resistance refers to the amount of energy required for a tire to roll across the road. Tires with lower rolling resistance can help vehicles use less gasoline or electricity.

Under the first phase of California’s program, replacement tires will have to meet a maximum rolling-resistance level of 9.1 newtons per kilonewton beginning in 2029. The standard becomes stricter in 2033, when the maximum drops to 7.2 N/kN.

The CEC says the program is intended to ensure that replacement tires sold in the state are, on average, at least as energy efficient as the tires originally supplied with new passenger vehicles and light-duty trucks.

The commission estimates that the program could eventually save California drivers close to $1 billion annually in fuel and electricity expenses while reducing carbon dioxide emissions by approximately 2 million metric tons each year.

Tire Prices Become a Major Point of Debate

The regulations have drawn criticism from portions of the tire industry, which argue that meeting the new standards could increase consumer costs and reduce the number of tire choices available.

The CEC estimates the additional cost will average approximately $1.50 per tire during the first phase and $6.50 per tire after the stricter requirements take effect.

Industry representatives have disputed those projections. Goodyear and other manufacturers have raised concerns that the impact could be substantially higher, particularly once the second phase begins.

KCRA reported that Goodyear estimates the rules could eliminate roughly 70% of the tires currently sold in California by 2033. The CEC has acknowledged that a significant portion of today’s replacement-tire market will not meet the future standards.

Not Every Tire Will Be Covered

California’s program includes exemptions for a number of specialty products.

Among the exclusions are certain motorsports tires, snow tires, winter-performance tires, used and retreaded tires, deep-tread tires, motorcycle tires, temporary spare tires, certain off-road tires and limited-production tires.

The rules also contain exemptions for certain emergency-vehicle tires, high-load-index tires and other specialized products.

That means the regulations do not amount to an outright prohibition on every type of replacement tire.

Environmental Benefits Cited by State Officials

California officials and environmental advocates have emphasized the potential fuel savings and emissions reductions.

The CEC estimates that a typical gasoline vehicle could save approximately $179 in fuel over the life of a set of more efficient tires. The commission says those savings could be greater under elevated gasoline prices.

Michelin has publicly supported the efficiency targets, saying the requirements are technically achievable while maintaining safety and other important tire characteristics.

The CEC also says it tested hundreds of tire models and reviewed industry data before establishing the standards.

The Rules Took More Than Two Decades to Reach the Finish Line

The new program traces its origins to legislation passed in 2003.

Assembly Bill 844 directed California to develop standards addressing tire efficiency. However, the state spent years working through the regulatory process before formally establishing the program.

The CEC says it conducted extensive discussions with manufacturers and industry organizations during the development of the regulations.

The commission says it will continue working with manufacturers and retailers as the requirements are implemented.

What California Drivers Should Know

Drivers do not have to immediately replace their tires because of the new regulations. The first phase does not begin until 2029, and the stricter requirements will not take effect until 2033.

For consumers, the biggest changes are expected to involve the selection of replacement tires available through California retailers.

The state says the program is intended to reduce fuel and electricity consumption without compromising safety or tire life. Industry opponents, meanwhile, have questioned the projected costs and the potential reduction in available tire choices.

With the first requirements still several years away, manufacturers will have time to modify existing products and introduce replacement tires designed to meet California’s standards.
